# A Corporate Finance Perspective on Value-Based Healthcare: A Research Agenda for Beveridgean Systems

## Part I: The Theoretical Nexus of Finance, Value, and Public Health

### Section 1: The Healthcare Provider as a Financial Entity in a State-Monopsony System

The transition to Value-Based Healthcare (VBHC) within tax-funded, single-payer health systems presents a profound strategic and financial challenge. These systems, often categorized under the Beveridge model, are characterized by healthcare provision and financing managed by the government through tax revenues.1 This structure grants the state significant power as the sole or primary payer, which historically has been used to control costs and ensure universal access.1 The Portuguese Serviço Nacional de Saúde (SNS) serves as a quintessential example of this model. Established in 1979, the SNS offers universal healthcare coverage funded through general taxation, encompassing a network of public and private providers. While universal, it is not a system of comprehensive, free-at-the-point-of-use coverage for all services, incorporating cost-sharing mechanisms, particularly for pharmaceutical products.3

This state-monopsony environment creates a unique financial context for healthcare providers, such as public hospitals within the SNS. These organizations are not conventional corporations with profit-maximizing objectives and access to capital markets. Instead, they operate as complex quasi-corporate entities, caught within a fundamental financial dichotomy. On one hand, their financial existence is dictated by a top-down, hierarchical bureaucracy. The central administration, in Portugal's case the Administração Central do Sistema de Saúde (ACSS), is responsible for setting budgets, while individual hospitals retain significant, albeit constrained, power over the allocation of these funds.3 This structure, often described as a pyramid with primary care at the base and high-tech hospitals at the apex, imposes rigid financial controls and limits operational autonomy.4

On the other hand, the advent of VBHC imposes a new set of expectations that are more aligned with the dynamic behavior of a competitive firm. VBHC models demand that providers move beyond simply delivering services to actively managing population health, improving patient outcomes, and generating "value"—defined as health outcomes relative to the cost of delivering them.3 This requires a capacity for rapid learning, process innovation, and strategic investment—an "organizational nimbleness" that is often at odds with the traditional bureaucratic structure.6 The core tension lies in the misalignment between the financial architecture of the Beveridgean system and the investment logic demanded by VBHC. The transition to value-based models requires substantial, often risky, upfront capital expenditures in data infrastructure, new care delivery models, and preventative health programs.7 The financial returns on these investments are typically delayed, often taking several years to materialize in the form of shared savings or performance bonuses, a pattern that can negatively impact margins in the short to medium term.8 This long-term investment horizon clashes with the typical annual operational budget cycles of public healthcare systems, which are designed for stability and cost containment rather than for financing multi-year strategic transformations. This fundamental friction between the financial logic of VBHC and the financial architecture of the provider forms the central challenge that corporate finance theory must address.

### Section 2: Re-Tooling Corporate Finance for the Value-Based Healthcare Revolution

The principles of Advanced Corporate Finance, typically applied to publicly traded corporations, offer a powerful yet underutilized toolkit for navigating the complexities of the VBHC transition in public health systems. The core tenets of capital budgeting, capital structure, valuation, and agency theory, as outlined in representative advanced finance syllabi 9, must be re-tooled and adapted to this unique environment. The availability of vast, longitudinal health data transforms these financial concepts from theoretical exercises into practical instruments for strategic decision-making.

**Capital Budgeting Beyond Net Present Value (NPV):** Traditional capital budgeting techniques, such as NPV, are often inadequate for evaluating VBHC investments. Syllabi for advanced finance courses emphasize investment decision-making under uncertainty, and VBHC projects are the epitome of this challenge.10 An investment in a predictive analytics platform to identify at-risk patients, for example, has an uncertain payoff that depends on technology adoption, clinician behavior change, and data quality.5 A standard NPV analysis would likely undervalue such a project by failing to capture the value of managerial flexibility. Real Options Analysis, a topic explicitly covered in some corporate finance curricula 11, provides a more appropriate framework. It allows decision-makers to value the "options" embedded in a project: the option to expand a successful pilot program, the option to abandon a technology that fails to deliver results, or the option to wait for greater data interoperability before committing to a full-scale rollout. These options have significant economic value but are ignored in a static NPV calculation.

**Capital Structure and the Cost of Risk:** Corporate finance theory is deeply concerned with a firm's capital structure—its mix of debt and equity—and the resulting cost of capital.9 While a public hospital has no "equity" in the traditional sense, the principles of risk and return remain paramount. VBHC models, particularly those involving downside risk such as shared savings or bundled payments, fundamentally alter the provider's risk profile.13 By accepting these contracts, the provider effectively takes on financial risk that was previously borne by the state payer. This increased risk exposure is a primary barrier to adoption, with a majority of providers citing financial risk as their top concern.7 A critical task for financial analysis is to quantify this risk and incorporate it into a "cost of capital" for VBHC projects. This allows for a more rational comparison of different value-based contracts and investments, ensuring that the potential returns are sufficient to compensate for the risks undertaken.

**Valuation of Intangible Assets: The Data Imperative:** A central theme in corporate finance is the valuation of firms and their assets.11 In the context of a national health service, the most valuable and least understood asset is data. The Portuguese NHS, for example, possesses an "ever increasing and comprehensive data on people's health" that represents a "unique source of value".16 This repository of longitudinal health data is a classic intangible asset. To justify the significant investments required for data governance, analytics, and security, it is essential to assign a credible financial value to this asset. Corporate finance offers methodologies—such as the relief-from-royalty method or the multi-period excess earnings method, typically used for patents and brands—that can be adapted to value a health data repository based on its potential to generate value through improved public health intelligence, more efficient clinical trials, and the development of new care pathways.17

**Agency Theory and Performance Measurement:** Agency theory, which examines the conflicts of interest between principals (e.g., shareholders) and agents (e.g., managers), is a cornerstone of corporate governance studies.10 The entire premise of VBHC is to resolve the core agency problem in healthcare: the misalignment between the fee-for-service model, which incentivizes the agent (provider) to maximize volume, and the desire of the principal (patient and payer) for better health outcomes at a reasonable cost. However, VBHC introduces new, more subtle agency problems. Performance-based payments can lead to "teaching to the test," where providers focus on improving a few narrow, incentivized metrics at the expense of overall patient care.6 The challenge is to design performance management systems (PMSs) and compensation contracts that align the incentives of clinicians and managers with the long-term goal of value creation. This requires moving beyond simple process measures to incorporate sophisticated, risk-adjusted metrics such as Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs), which capture the patient's own perspective on their health and care.21

The following table provides a systematic mapping of these core corporate finance concepts to the specific challenges and data requirements of implementing VBHC in a Beveridgean system.

|---|---|---|---|
|**Real Options Analysis**|Valuing R&D pipelines, mining exploration rights, or phased manufacturing plant expansions.|Valuing staged investments in telehealth infrastructure, AI-driven diagnostic tools, or a national electronic health record system, explicitly modeling the value of the option to expand, contract, or abandon the project based on interim results.|Longitudinal cost and outcome data for pilot programs; data on technology adoption rates and forecasts; evidence on the need for upgraded health IT and analytics.5|

This re-framing demonstrates that the successful implementation of VBHC is not merely a clinical or policy challenge; it is fundamentally a corporate finance challenge that requires a sophisticated application of financial principles to a new and complex domain.

### Section 3: Mapping the Research Frontier: Financial and Strategic Gaps in VBHC Implementation

A comprehensive review of the current literature reveals that despite more than a decade of discussion and experimentation, the global transition to VBHC remains in its infancy. The existing research clearly delineates a set of critical, interconnected gaps that hinder progress, particularly within the context of large, publicly-funded health systems. These gaps are not primarily clinical but are rooted in finance, strategy, and information management, forming a clear agenda for future research.

**The Scaling and Implementation Gap:** The most prominent finding from recent scoping reviews is that VBHC implementation is overwhelmingly small-scale and fragmented. Initiatives are typically confined to the departmental or institutional level, often as isolated pilot projects within a single hospital or for a specific disease condition.24 There is a stark absence of evidence of coordinated, large-scale, system-level implementation designed to create a truly high-value health system. Even in countries with national VBHC programs, the execution remains primarily at the departmental level.24 This points to a critical research gap: the lack of proven financial and strategic models for scaling VBHC from isolated pockets of excellence to the default operating model of an entire national health service.

**The Financial Sustainability Gap:** A deep-seated concern pervades the literature regarding the long-term financial viability of the VBHC transition for providers. The model requires significant upfront investment, and the path to a positive return is often long and uncertain. Analysis suggests that the transition to risk-based contracting can depress a health system's margins for several years before recovery and growth are possible.8 Furthermore, some studies have found that value-based reimbursement agreements, after accounting for financial bonuses and administrative overhead, can lead to an _increase_ in total healthcare spending, contrary to their primary objective.25 The central unanswered question is how to engineer financing and reimbursement models that ensure the long-term financial sustainability of providers, enabling them to make the necessary investments without jeopardizing their solvency. The current focus on generating savings for the payer often overlooks the critical need for a viable business case for the provider.25

**The Performance Measurement and "Value" Definition Gap:** While the conceptual definition of value (Outcomes/Cost) is widely cited, there is "no single methodology...to measure and evaluate its impact" in practice.21 Many value-based purchasing programs lack specific, quantifiable goals, making it nearly impossible to determine if they are successful.6 This ambiguity leads to a reliance on a narrow set of process-based quality measures that are easily collected but may not correlate with the outcomes that matter most to patients. This creates a significant risk of providers "teaching to the test"—optimizing performance on the incentivized metrics while potentially neglecting other crucial aspects of care.20 The research gap lies in developing and validating holistic, risk-adjusted performance measurement systems that capture the multidimensional nature of "value," integrating clinical outcomes, patient-reported outcomes (PROMs), and patient experiences (PREMs) in a meaningful way.22

**The Data-to-Value Gap:** National health systems like Portugal's are data-rich, possessing vast repositories of longitudinal health information that are recognized as a key source of potential value.16 However, a significant gap exists between the potential of this data and its actual use in creating measurable financial and clinical value. Key barriers include a lack of data interoperability between different systems, which hinders the creation of a comprehensive patient view, and the administrative complexity of integrating new data-driven workflows.7 Furthermore, realizing the value of this data requires maintaining public trust in its safe and confidential use, a challenge compounded by regulations like the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).17 The research gap is in identifying and testing the specific financial, organizational, and governance models needed to bridge this chasm—to translate raw health data into actionable insights that improve care and reduce costs.

These identified gaps are not independent but form a reinforcing cycle of failure. The absence of robust, meaningful performance metrics (Gap 3) makes it impossible to design effective risk-sharing contracts and reimbursement models. This, in turn, creates profound uncertainty about financial sustainability (Gap 2). This financial uncertainty and risk aversion prevent providers from committing the capital necessary for large-scale transformation, thus explaining the persistent failure to scale (Gap 1). At the root of this entire chain is the inability to effectively translate data into measurable value (Gap 4), which is the essential raw material for performance measurement, risk modeling, and investment justification. The failure to advance VBHC is, therefore, not a failure of clinical ambition but a failure of financial engineering and information management. This reframing provides the intellectual foundation for a new research agenda focused on building the financial and data architecture required to support a truly value-based health system.

## Part II: Ten Original Research Proposals for a Q1 Management Journal

The following ten research proposals are designed to address the critical gaps identified at the intersection of corporate finance, data analytics, and value-based healthcare in Beveridgean systems. Each topic is formulated to be of sufficient novelty and rigor for a top-tier management journal, offering an "out-of-the-box" solution or perspective. The proposals are organized into three thematic clusters: Valuation and Capital Budgeting, Innovative Financing and Capital Structure, and Corporate Governance and Risk Management.

### Cluster A: Valuation and Capital Budgeting in a Data-Rich Environment

This cluster focuses on applying advanced valuation techniques to justify and prioritize the substantial investments required for the VBHC transition, challenging the static, accounting-based logic of traditional public sector budgeting.

#### Topic 1: Valuing Strategic Flexibility in Health IT Investments: A Real Options Approach to a National Health Data Lake

- **Problem Statement:** Public health systems face immense pressure to invest in large-scale data infrastructure, such as the "HEALTH DATA SPACE" envisioned for Portugal's NHS.17 Traditional capital budgeting methods like NPV fail to capture the primary source of value in these projects: the strategic flexibility they create for the future. The uncertainty surrounding future AI applications, data standards, and secondary use cases makes a static cash flow projection highly unreliable.
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research would be grounded in Real Options Analysis, treating the investment in a foundational data platform not as a single, irreversible decision, but as the acquisition of a portfolio of growth options.11 The initial investment is analogous to paying a premium for a call option on future data-driven innovations.
    
- **Novel Research Question:** How can a Real Options framework be used to model and quantify the value of strategic flexibility (e.g., the option to expand into predictive analytics, the option to enter public-private data-sharing partnerships, the option to defer adoption of specific AI tools) embedded in a national health data infrastructure investment?
    
- **Contribution:** This paper would provide a theoretically sound and practically applicable model for justifying and prioritizing large-scale digital health investments in the public sector. By quantifying the value of flexibility, it would offer a powerful counter-narrative to short-term, cost-focused budgeting, enabling policymakers to make more strategically astute capital allocation decisions.
    

#### Topic 2: Beyond Cost Savings: A Multi-Attribute Framework for Valuing a Public Health Data Repository as a Strategic Asset

- **Problem Statement:** While it is widely acknowledged that national health data repositories are a "unique source of value" 16, there is no accepted methodology for quantifying this value beyond simple cost-avoidance calculations. This undervaluation leads to underinvestment in data governance, quality, and security.
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research would draw from the corporate finance literature on the valuation of intangible assets, combining elements of the income approach (e.g., potential licensing revenue for anonymized data for clinical trials 18), the market approach (e.g., benchmarking against private sector data transactions), and non-financial valuation frameworks (e.g., balanced scorecard).
    
- **Novel Research Question:** What is the comprehensive economic value of a national health data repository when valued as a multi-purpose strategic asset, incorporating its utility for (a) accelerating clinical research, (b) improving public health surveillance and policy-making, (c) enabling personalized medicine at scale, and (d) fostering a domestic health-tech innovation ecosystem?
    
- **Contribution:** This study would produce the first comprehensive, multi-attribute valuation framework for a public health data asset. Such a framework would provide a robust financial justification for public investment in data infrastructure and would create a basis for designing governance models that maximize the asset's societal and economic returns while maintaining public trust.17
    

#### Topic 3: The Capital Budgeting of Prevention: A Social Return on Investment (SROI) Model for Value-Based Healthcare

- **Problem Statement:** A core principle of VBHC is a shift towards prevention and early intervention to avoid costly complications later.27 However, capital budgeting for preventative programs (e.g., diabetes prevention, smoking cessation) is notoriously difficult because the costs are immediate and concentrated, while the benefits are long-term, diffuse, and accrue to multiple stakeholders (patients, the health system, employers, society).
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research will integrate the principles of Social Return on Investment (SROI) with the rigor of corporate finance capital budgeting. It will use financial valuation techniques to assign a monetary value to the social and health outcomes generated, and then discount these future value streams using a socially-adjusted discount rate to calculate a risk-adjusted SROI ratio.
    
- **Novel Research Question:** How can a hybrid SROI-NPV model be constructed to provide a robust business case for long-term preventative health investments within a Beveridgean system, accounting for the time value of money, the risk of program failure, and the distribution of value across multiple societal stakeholders?
    
- **Contribution:** This paper would provide a practical and defensible methodology for public health administrators to make evidence-based investment decisions in preventative care. By translating long-term health benefits into a compelling financial metric, it would help overcome the inherent short-termism of annual budget cycles and facilitate the strategic shift from a treatment-focused to a prevention-focused health system.
    

### Cluster B: Innovative Financing and Capital Structure for the VBHC Transition

This cluster focuses on designing novel financial instruments and capital structures to overcome the critical barriers of cash flow mismatch and risk aversion that currently hinder the widespread adoption of VBHC.

#### Topic 4: Securitizing Value: The Design and Financial Impact of Value-Based Receivables Financing

- **Problem Statement:** A major financial barrier for providers in VBHC contracts is the significant lag between incurring the costs of care transformation and receiving performance-based revenues like shared savings, which can trail by a year or more.7 This cash flow mismatch strains working capital and discourages participation.
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research is grounded in the principles of structured finance and securitization. It builds on the concept of "receivable financing" 7 by designing a specific financial instrument: a bond or note backed by a diversified pool of future, uncertain shared-savings receivables from a group of public hospitals.
    
- **Novel Research Question:** What is the optimal structure for a special purpose vehicle (SPV) designed to purchase and securitize future shared-savings receivables from public hospitals in a Beveridgean system? How would the sale of these receivables impact the hospitals' balance sheets, risk profiles, and ability to invest in further care transformation initiatives?
    
- **Contribution:** This study would move beyond the conceptual idea of receivables financing to offer a detailed blueprint for a new financial product tailored to the public healthcare sector. It would demonstrate how financial engineering can solve a critical operational problem, providing hospitals with immediate liquidity to fund the VBHC transition and transferring the timing risk to capital market investors.
    

#### Topic 5: De-Risking the Transition: A Public-Private Blended Finance Model for VBHC Infrastructure

- **Problem Statement:** The scale of investment required for a system-wide VBHC transition (e.g., in data analytics, telehealth, and integrated care facilities) often exceeds the capacity of public budgets alone. Private capital is available but is deterred by the high perceived risks and long payback periods associated with public sector health projects.
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research will draw on the literature of development finance and infrastructure investing, specifically on blended finance models. It will propose a structure where public funds or philanthropic capital are used in a "first-loss" or guarantee capacity to de-risk the investment, thereby "crowding in" private capital from institutional investors (e.g., pension funds, impact investors) at a lower cost.
    
- **Novel Research Question:** What is the optimal capital structure for a blended finance facility aimed at funding VBHC infrastructure in a Beveridgean system? How can public guarantees and subordinated capital be calibrated to attract the maximum amount of private investment while ensuring that the public interest and patient value remain the primary objectives?
    
- **Contribution:** This paper would provide a concrete financial model for overcoming the capital constraints of public health systems. By designing a symbiotic relationship between public and private capital, it would unlock new funding sources for the VBHC transition, accelerating the pace of change beyond what is possible with tax revenues alone.7
    

#### Topic 6: Quantifying the Cost of Risk: A Synthetic Cost of Capital for Public Providers in Two-Sided Risk Models

- **Problem Statement:** When a public hospital considers a VBHC contract with downside risk, it lacks a key decision-making tool available to private firms: a risk-adjusted discount rate or cost of capital. Without it, the decision to accept or reject the contract is based on intuition rather than rigorous financial analysis, contributing to the widespread risk aversion observed in the sector.7
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research will use principles from corporate finance and portfolio theory to develop a synthetic cost of capital model for non-profit, public entities. The model would not be based on market returns but would be built up from a risk-free rate plus a quantifiable risk premium derived from the specific parameters of the value-based contract (e.g., the size of the potential penalty, the volatility of patient costs, the correlation with other hospital activities).
    
- **Novel Research Question:** How can a synthetic, contract-specific cost of capital be calculated for a public healthcare provider, and how does its application in project evaluation change the optimal selection of value-based contracts compared to traditional, non-risk-adjusted budgeting approaches?
    
- **Contribution:** This study would introduce a crucial element of financial discipline to the adoption of VBHC. By providing a tool to rationally price risk, it would empower hospital CFOs to distinguish between attractive and unattractive risk-sharing arrangements, leading to more sustainable and financially sound participation in value-based models.
    

### Cluster C: Corporate Governance, Risk Management, and Performance

This cluster examines the critical internal organizational changes—in governance, incentives, and risk management—that are prerequisites for a successful and sustainable VBHC strategy.

#### Topic 7: Reforming Hospital Governance for a Data-Driven Era: The Case for a Board-Level "Data & Value Committee"

- **Problem Statement:** The strategic management of data and the oversight of complex value-based contracts represent a new and profound challenge for public hospital boards, whose expertise traditionally lies in clinical quality, public administration, and basic finance. This governance gap can lead to a failure to capitalize on data as a strategic asset and an inability to effectively oversee the financial risks of VBHC.
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research will apply theories of corporate governance and board effectiveness to the public hospital context.10 It will argue that just as the audit committee became standard in response to financial reporting scandals, a new governance structure is needed to address the strategic imperatives of the digital and value-based era.
    
- **Novel Research Question:** What should be the mandate, composition, and key performance indicators of a specialized Board-level "Data & Value Committee" within a public hospital? How would the existence of such a committee impact the quality of strategic decision-making related to data analytics investments and the management of value-based risk contracts?
    
- **Contribution:** This paper would propose a concrete and actionable reform to public hospital governance. It would provide a charter for a new committee structure designed to bridge the gap between the boardroom and the data science team, ensuring that data governance and value-based strategy receive the expert oversight they require.17
    

#### Topic 8: Beyond "Teaching to the Test": Optimal Incentive Design in Integrated Practice Units

- **Problem Statement:** While performance incentives are central to VBHC, poorly designed schemes can lead to unintended negative consequences, such as focusing on narrow metrics or avoiding complex patients.20 This is a classic agency problem. The challenge is particularly acute in Integrated Practice Units (IPUs)—multidisciplinary teams organized around a patient's condition—which are a cornerstone of the VBHC model.27
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research will draw heavily on agency theory and contract theory. It will model the clinical team as an "agent" and the health system as the "principal" and seek to design an "optimal contract" that minimizes agency costs.
    
- **Novel Research Question:** What is the optimal structure of a multi-period incentive contract for a clinical IPU that effectively balances (a) short-term process metrics, (b) long-term, risk-adjusted patient-reported outcomes (PROMs), and (c) team-based versus individual rewards, in order to maximize patient value while mitigating the risks of "teaching to the test"?
    
- **Contribution:** This study would provide a theoretically grounded, evidence-based template for designing next-generation clinical incentive plans. By moving beyond simple pay-for-performance, it would offer a sophisticated solution to one of the most persistent challenges in VBHC implementation, helping to ensure that financial incentives truly align with the goal of better patient health.
    

#### Topic 9: A Portfolio Approach to Value-Based Contracting: Developing a Financial Risk Dashboard for Hospital CFOs

- **Problem Statement:** As hospitals move away from a single fee-for-service payment model, their CFOs will need to manage a complex portfolio of value-based contracts, each with a different risk-reward profile (e.g., upside-only shared savings, bundled payments with downside risk, full capitation). Traditional hospital financial reporting is ill-equipped to monitor and manage this new portfolio of risks.
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research will apply principles of financial risk management and portfolio theory, as taught in advanced corporate finance.9 It will treat the hospital's set of value-based contracts as a financial portfolio and develop metrics to measure its overall risk exposure, diversification benefits, and risk-adjusted return.
    
- **Novel Research Question:** What are the key quantitative metrics (e.g., Value at Risk (VaR), downside deviation, correlation of outcomes) that should be included in a "Financial Risk Dashboard" for a hospital CFO to effectively manage the aggregate risk of a portfolio of diverse value-based contracts?
    
- **Contribution:** This paper would create a practical management tool to help hospital finance leaders navigate the transition to VBHC. The dashboard would enable them to move from a contract-by-contract view to a holistic, portfolio-level understanding of their financial risk, facilitating more strategic decisions about which contracts to pursue and how to hedge their exposures.
    

#### Topic 10: The Value-Based "Make-or-Buy" Decision: A Financial Model for Outsourcing in Public Health Systems

- **Problem Statement:** Some scholars have proposed that public health systems could achieve value by outsourcing certain services to private providers operating under a value-based contract.28 This raises a classic corporate finance question: when is it more value-accretive for the system to "make" (provide a service internally under a new VBHC model) versus "buy" (contract with an external expert provider)?
    
- **Theoretical Grounding:** This research will be based on transaction cost economics and the corporate finance literature on outsourcing and vertical integration. It will develop a financial model that goes beyond a simple comparison of costs to include factors like quality control, risk transfer, and the potential for innovation.
    
- **Novel Research Question:** What is the comprehensive financial framework that a public health system like the SNS should use to make a "make-or-buy" decision for a specific clinical service line (e.g., joint replacement, cataract surgery)? The framework must compare the total risk-adjusted cost of internal transformation with the price and terms of a value-based contract with an external provider.
    
- **Contribution:** This study would provide a rigorous, data-driven framework for one of the most politically and strategically sensitive decisions in public healthcare. It would replace ideological debate with financial analysis, enabling policymakers to determine, on a case-by-case basis, the most efficient and effective way to deliver value to patients, whether through internal reform or strategic outsourcing.
    

## Part III: Synthesis and A Forward-Looking Agenda

### Section 4: Toward a Unified Framework for Financial Strategy in Value-Based Beveridgean Systems

The ten research proposals, while distinct, collectively point toward the necessity of a new, integrated strategic framework for financial management in public healthcare. The traditional, siloed approach where clinical governance, financial management, and information technology strategy operate in separate spheres is no longer viable in an era defined by data and value. The successful navigation of the VBHC transition requires a unified **"Data-Driven Value-Based Financial Strategy"** that breaks down these organizational barriers and reorients the entire provider organization around the singular goal of maximizing patient value per unit of cost. This framework rests on three core pillars.

First is **Value-Centric Capital Allocation**. This pillar represents a fundamental shift away from incremental, budget-based allocation towards a dynamic portfolio management approach to investment. In this model, all potential investments—whether in new clinical technologies, preventative care programs, or data infrastructure—are evaluated and prioritized based on their risk-adjusted contribution to the core value equation. This requires the systematic application of advanced financial tools like Real Options Analysis to value flexibility and SROI to quantify long-term social benefits. The organization's capital is treated as a scarce resource to be deployed to its highest value-creating use, transforming the budget from a ceiling on spending into a tool for strategic investment.

Second is **Dynamic Financial Engineering**. This pillar recognizes that the financial structure of a public provider must become as adaptable as its clinical strategies. It involves moving beyond the passive acceptance of centrally allocated budgets to the active management of the organization's capital structure and risk profile. The toolkit for this pillar includes the innovative financing instruments explored in the research proposals, such as value-based receivables financing to manage working capital, and blended public-private finance models to access new pools of capital for large-scale transformation. It also requires the development of sophisticated internal capabilities, such as the ability to calculate a synthetic cost of capital for risk-based contracts, enabling the organization to proactively shape its financial destiny rather than merely react to it.

The third and final pillar is **Integrated Governance and Incentives**. This pillar addresses the human and organizational dimensions of the strategy. It posits that financial and data strategies cannot succeed without a corresponding evolution in governance and motivation. This involves reforming board oversight to include deep expertise in data and financial risk, potentially through new structures like a "Data & Value Committee." Crucially, it requires the redesign of performance management and compensation systems at every level—from the executive suite to the clinical frontline—to align incentives with the long-term, data-driven creation of patient value. By creating incentive contracts that balance short-term process goals with long-term patient outcomes, this pillar seeks to solve the agency problems that have long plagued healthcare and ensure that the entire organization is pulling in the direction of value.

Together, these three pillars form a cohesive and actionable framework. They argue that for a public healthcare provider in a Beveridgean system, financial strategy is no longer a back-office function concerned with balancing the books. It is the central nervous system that connects data to decisions, capital to outcomes, and incentives to purpose.

### Section 5: Navigating the Empirical Terrain: Methodological Pathways and Data Considerations

Conducting high-impact empirical research on these topics requires a pragmatic approach to the significant methodological and data-related challenges inherent in the healthcare sector. A clear understanding of these challenges is essential for designing a feasible and rigorous doctoral research program.

**Data Access and Governance:** The primary challenge for any empirical study in this domain is gaining access to the necessary data. Research will require granular, patient-level data on costs, clinical processes, and outcomes, linked with financial data from provider organizations. While systems like the Portuguese SNS are data-rich, accessing this information is complex. Researchers must navigate the strategic initiatives aimed at promoting the secondary use of health data 16 while strictly adhering to the legal and ethical frameworks governing sensitive personal information, most notably the EU's GDPR.17 Successful research will likely depend on establishing formal partnerships with health authorities or specific provider organizations, potentially working within secure data environments or "safe havens" that allow for analysis without compromising patient privacy.

**Methodological Approaches:** Given the complexity and nascent stage of VBHC implementation 24, a single methodological approach is unlikely to be sufficient. A mixed-methods research design is strongly recommended. Quantitative methods, such as econometric analysis of large administrative datasets, will be essential for testing the financial models proposed (e.g., the impact of new incentive contracts or the pricing of value-based receivables). However, quantitative data alone cannot explain the "how" and "why" of the VBHC transition. Qualitative methods are indispensable for understanding the nuanced processes of organizational change. In-depth case studies of pioneering hospitals or IPUs 27, supplemented by semi-structured interviews with key stakeholders—hospital CFOs, clinical leaders, health system policymakers, and financial innovators—will be crucial for building rich, theory-grounded explanations of the successes and failures observed in the data.

**Addressing International Comparability:** The research literature explicitly warns of the pitfalls of international comparisons of health systems, which are often confounded by fundamental differences in national definitions and data collection methods.4 While the ultimate goal may be to generate universally applicable insights, the most robust initial research strategy is to focus deeply on a single, well-defined system, such as the Portuguese SNS. By developing and testing theories within a consistent institutional and data context, a researcher can build a solid foundation of evidence. This single-system deep-dive approach minimizes confounding variables and allows for a more nuanced understanding of the causal mechanisms at play. The validated models and theories developed from this focused research can then serve as a more reliable basis for carefully constructed, subsequent cross-country comparative studies.
