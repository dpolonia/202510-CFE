### **Topic 3: The Capital Budgeting of Prevention: A Social Return on Investment (SROI) Model for Value-Based Healthcare**

- **Research Rationale and Problem Statement:** A foundational principle of Value-Based Healthcare (VBHC) is the strategic shift from reactive treatment to proactive prevention and early intervention, aiming to avoid costly and debilitating health complications down the line.1 However, this strategic imperative is consistently undermined by a fundamental capital budgeting problem. Preventative health programs (e.g., national diabetes prevention, smoking cessation, community-based mental health support) are characterized by immediate, concentrated costs, while their benefits are long-term, diffuse, and accrue to a wide range of stakeholders beyond the healthcare system itself—including patients, employers, and society at large.2
    
    This creates a classic "wrong pocket" problem: the entity making the investment (the public health system) does not capture the full spectrum of returns, such as increased workforce productivity or reduced social welfare costs.2 Traditional financial evaluation methods used in healthcare capital budgeting, such as payback period or simple ROI, are designed for projects with clear, direct financial returns over a relatively short horizon and are therefore inadequate for valuing prevention.4 This methodological gap leads to a systematic under-investment in high-value preventative care, despite its potential to save both lives and money in the long run.6
    
- **Theoretical Framework:** This research will develop a hybrid valuation model that integrates the holistic, stakeholder-centric approach of **Social Return on Investment (SROI)** with the financial rigor of **corporate finance capital budgeting**.
    
    - **Social Return on Investment (SROI):** SROI is a framework for measuring and accounting for the broad social, economic, and environmental value created by an intervention relative to the resources invested.2 It moves beyond simple financial metrics by engaging with stakeholders to identify all relevant outcomes (positive, negative, intended, and unintended) and then assigning credible financial proxies to represent their value.11 The final output is typically an SROI ratio (e.g., 3:1), indicating that every €1 invested generates €3 of social value.11 SROI is increasingly used to evaluate public health interventions precisely because it can capture the wider value that traditional economic evaluations often miss.13
        
    - **Corporate Finance Capital Budgeting:** While SROI excels at capturing a broad definition of value, it will be enhanced by incorporating core principles from corporate finance, namely the **time value of money** and **risk analysis**. The model will discount the future streams of social and financial benefits using a **socially-adjusted discount rate**, which reflects society's time preference and the specific risk profile of the preventative program. This creates a risk-adjusted SROI ratio or a hybrid "Social NPV" that is more robust and comparable to other long-term capital projects.
        
- **Key Research Questions:**
    
    1. How can a capital budgeting model systematically identify and value the benefits of a preventative health program that accrue to multiple stakeholders (e.g., the health system, patients, employers, and the state) to overcome the "wrong pocket" problem?
        
    2. What is a robust and defensible methodology for assigning financial proxies to the key non-market outcomes of preventative care, such as improved mental well-being, enhanced quality of life, and increased social cohesion?
        
    3. How can a socially-adjusted discount rate be constructed for public health investments that appropriately accounts for long-term societal benefits and the inherent uncertainties of preventative interventions?
        
    4. By how much does the valuation of a major preventative health investment change when assessed using a hybrid SROI-NPV model compared to traditional methods that focus only on direct healthcare cost savings?
        
- **Proposed Methodology:**
    
    - **Model Construction:** The initial phase will involve the theoretical development of the hybrid SROI-NPV framework, defining the integration of SROI's six stages (scoping, mapping outcomes, evidencing and valuing outcomes, establishing impact, calculation, and reporting) 12 within a discounted valuation model.
        
    - **Case Study Application:** The model will be applied to a real-world, large-scale preventative health program within a Beveridgean system, such as Portugal's national vaccination program or a major public health campaign aimed at reducing obesity.
        
    - **Mixed-Methods Data Collection:**
        
        - **Qualitative:** A cornerstone of the SROI methodology is deep stakeholder engagement.12 This will involve semi-structured interviews and focus groups with patients, clinicians, public health officials, and representatives from employer groups to build a "Theory of Change" map that identifies all the material outcomes of the intervention.11
            
        - **Quantitative:** Financial data on the program's costs will be collected from the health system. The valuation of outcomes will be based on financial proxies derived from a systematic review of economic literature, government statistics, and health economics databases. This data will be used to populate the quantitative model and calculate the final SROI-NPV.
            
- **Expected Contribution:**
    
    - **For Theory:** This research will create a novel synthesis of social accounting (SROI) and corporate finance theory, offering a more sophisticated and appropriate framework for valuing public sector investments that generate significant social externalities.
        
    - **For Practice:** The primary contribution is a powerful, practical, and defensible tool for policymakers and public health administrators. By translating the long-term, multi-faceted benefits of prevention into a rigorous, risk-adjusted financial metric, the model provides a compelling business case to justify these investments. It directly counters the short-termism inherent in annual government budget cycles 5 and provides the evidence needed to strategically reallocate resources from costly treatment to high-value prevention.
