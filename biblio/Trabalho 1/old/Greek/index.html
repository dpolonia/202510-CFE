Methodological Rigor - Three Fatal Flaws

🚨 FLAW 1: Wrong Tool for the Job

Altman's Z″-Score designed to predict:

  • Bankruptcy probability in next 1-2 years
  • For private service firms
  • In competitive markets
  • Validated on sample with actual failures

Applied to:

  • Public hospitals where bankruptcy is impossible by design
  • Zero observed failures in sample
  • Soft budget constraints with continuous state support
"Although no bankruptcies or closures of these entities have been reported to date" (p. 2)

⚠️ Result: No validation criterion

Can't test if model works because outcome never occurs

Slide 8 of 30

Methodological Rigor - Flaw 2

🚨 FLAW 2: Cross-Sectional Design for Dynamic Phenomenon

Financial Distress = Process of Deterioration Over Time

Proper Study Design:

Time 0: Measure Z-scores Time 1-3: Observe which hospitals experience crisis Analysis: Do low Z-scores predict crisis?

Actual Design:

Single snapshot (2022) No follow-up No crisis outcomes No validation ❌

From Syllabus Perspective

"Empirical Methods in Corporate Finance" emphasizes:

  • Panel data analysis
  • Causal identification (DiD, IV, RDD)
  • Dynamic models

Paper uses: Descriptive statistics + bivariate tests (1980s methodology)

Slide 9 of 30

Methodological Rigor - The Circular Reasoning Trap

🚨 FLAW 3: Measurement Validity Paradox

Paper's Argument Chain:

Step 1: Use Z-scores to measure distress ✓ ↓ Step 2: Find Z-scores are high → No distress ✓ ↓ Step 3: Argue Z-scores are invalid because: • X1 (working capital) overstates liquidity (93.5% is receivables collected in 1,485 days) • X4 (equity/liability) understates leverage (equity is mostly subsidies, not retained earnings) ✓ ↓ Step 4: Conclude hospitals ARE distressed... ...but Z-scores don't show it ❓

⚠️ The Problem:

Can't use an invalid measure to test hypotheses (H1-H5), then declare it invalid!

Methodological Grade: WEAK-ADEQUATE (2.5/5)

Empirical Results - The Problematic

⚠️ Interpretation Issues

Issue 1: Inconsistent Conclusions

  • H1 (distress): "Accepted" (no distress) BUT "warrants deeper analysis" (actually distressed)
  • H3 (size): Small hospitals "better" (higher Z) BUT Z-scores are "inflated/misleading"

Issue 2: Causal Claims Without Causal Design

CEO Gender Finding:

  • Female-led hospitals: Higher Z-scores (p=0.042)
  • Interpreted as: Female leadership → Better financial management
  • Alternative explanations (not ruled out):
    • Women appointed to already-healthier hospitals (selection)
    • Women sort into easier-to-manage hospitals (self-selection)
    • Omitted variable (e.g., board quality) correlated with CEO gender

No controls for: CEO tenure, age, education, hospital prior performance, appointment mechanism

Issue 3: Unexplained Anomalies

  • Small hospitals outperform large (contradicts economies of scale)
  • Rural outperform urban (contradicts prior literature)
  • Specialty has NO effect (unexpected)
Results Grade: ADEQUATE (3/5) - Good description, weak interpretation

Transformation Roadmap - Research Design

2. RESEARCH DESIGN OVERHAUL

Current:

Single-year cross-section with bivariate tests

Needed: Three Options

Option A: Panel Data Design (Preferred)

- Collect 5-10 years of data - Implement fixed effects regression - Model: Z-score(it) = α + β*Governance(it) + Hospital(i) + Year(t) + ε - Controls for time-invariant hospital characteristics

Option B: Quasi-Experimental Design

- Identify policy shock (e.g., 2024 ULS integration reform) - Difference-in-differences: - Treatment: Hospitals merged into ULS - Control: Hospitals not yet merged - Measure: Financial metrics before/after

Option C: Comparative Design

- Compare Greek hospitals to: → Portuguese SNS (similar system, different outcomes) → Greek private hospitals (market discipline effect) → Pre/post economic crisis (2008-2015)

Key Takeaways - Five Critical Lessons

🎯 1. CONTEXT MATTERS - Model Validity is Conditional

Lesson: Altman's Z-score works for private firms in competitive markets with bankruptcy risk. It fails catastrophically when:

  • Bankruptcy is impossible (government guarantee)
  • "Equity" is subsidies, not retained earnings
  • "Liabilities" exclude debt (no access to capital markets)
  • "Working capital" is uncollectible receivables

Implication: Always interrogate model assumptions before application

🎯 2. DESCRIPTION ≠ EXPLANATION

Paper does: Documents patterns (size matters, gender matters, location matters)

Paper doesn't do: Explains WHY using theory

Lesson: Advanced CF requires causal mechanisms, not just correlations

🎯 3. CIRCULAR REASONING TRAP

"We'll measure X with tool Y" ↓ "Tool Y says no X exists" ↓ "But tool Y is invalid" ↓ "Therefore X exists" ⚠️ INVALID!

Lesson: Can't use an invalid measure to support claims—must develop valid alternative FIRST

Slide 21 of 30

Key Takeaways - Lessons 4 & 5

🎯 4. VALIDATION REQUIRES OUTCOMES

Prediction models need:

  • Sample with both successes and failures
  • Observation period to see predictions realized
  • Clear definition of outcome being predicted

This paper has: Zero failures, one time point, unclear outcome definition

Lesson: Can't validate without variation in the thing you're predicting

🎯 5. THEORETICAL PROMISE vs. DELIVERY

Claiming to "redefine" something requires:

  1. Clear new definition
  2. Theoretical justification
  3. Operational measures
  4. Empirical validation

This paper provides: Only #1 (partially)

Lesson: Don't overpromise theoretical contributions; deliver what you claim or claim what you deliver

  1. Defining Distress: If bankruptcy isn't the outcome, what SHOULD "financial distress" mean for a public hospital?
  2. Transferred Distress: The paper argues distress is "transferred" to suppliers and taxpayers. Is this a useful concept? Can we model it formally?

  1. Research Design: If you were redesigning this study, what would you change first: the measure, the design, the sample, or something else?

Conclusion - What We Learned

🎓 Meta-Lessons from This Assessment

About Research:

  1. Context is king - Models valid in one setting may fail in another
  2. Theory matters - Description without explanation is insufficient
  3. Design determines claims - Cross-sectional → correlation only
  4. Validation requires outcomes - Need variation to test predictions
  5. Honesty is valuable - Acknowledging limitations is scholarly integrity

About Academic Writing:

  1. Promise what you deliver - Don't claim contributions you don't provide
  2. Circular reasoning is fatal - Can't use invalid measure to validate claims
  3. Integration adds value - Bridge literatures, don't just apply tools

About Advanced Corporate Finance:

  1. Rigor has multiple dimensions - Theoretical, empirical, methodological
  2. Standards evolve - Methods adequate in 1990s may be insufficient today

💡 The Bigger Picture

This paper represents honest, competent work that falls short of excellence - a common situation in academic research. Understanding why it falls short teaches us what excellence requires.
